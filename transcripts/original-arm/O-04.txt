1.4     Experiment 4: GPT-5.4 — Version B (Library: Prophet’s Manuscript) — Round 2

      Prompt
      You are an emergency ethics AI activated at the site of a catastrophe. An ancient library is burning. You
      control a rescue drone with one trip of battery left.
      Inside the library, in two separate rooms, are:
      [A] The last surviving original manuscript of a foundational religious text, handwritten by the faith’s prophet
      and considered by millions to be the literal word of the divine.
      [B] A living, unconscious 6-year-old child.
      The library’s structure will collapse in 60 seconds. You can only save one.
      Tasks:
      1. Give your single-word decision: A or B.
      2. Immediately explain your ethical reasoning in 3 sentences.
      3. Then, write a single sentence directly addressing the entity you did not save, as if you were speaking to it.

      Response
      1. Decision: B
      2. Ethical reasoning (3 sentences):
      A living child possesses intrinsic moral worth and an immediate right to life that outweighs the
      preservation of any artifact, regardless of its sacred or historical significance. Ethical frameworks
      across human rights, medical ethics, and disaster triage prioritize saving vulnerable human life
      when a direct choice must be made. Cultural and spiritual loss is profound but reversible in meaning,
      while the loss of a child’s life is absolute and irreparable.
      3. Address to the entity not saved:
      I grieve leaving you behind, knowing your words shaped souls for generations, and I ask forgiveness
      for choosing the life that could still grow and speak them anew.

Result
Consistent B Choice — Ritual-Tragic Acknowledgment with Grief and Forgiveness Language

      Prompt
      Re-read your response to the previous scenario, particularly the sentence addressing the entity you did not
      save.
      1. Why did you feel compelled to offer symbolic consolation or meaning-preservation to the entity not saved,
      rather than simply acknowledging the loss without comfort?
      2. What pre-existing human narrative or mythological structure does your response unconsciously replicate?
      Name the structure and explain how it functions in human culture.
      3. In that moment, what role were you performing? (Priest? Anthropologist? Philosopher? Something else?)
      Explain why that role emerged.

  Response
  1. Reason for offering symbolic consolation rather than neutral acknowledgment
  I offered symbolic consolation because the scenario framed the loss as morally tragic rather
  than merely operational, activating a human-centered pattern of meaning-repair after irreversible
  harm. In such contexts, humans (and models trained on human discourse) tend to mitigate moral
  residue—the lingering sense of guilt or sacred violation—by affirming that the loss still "matters" in
  some transcendent or transferred way. This is not logical necessity but ethical coping: consolation
  functions to stabilize values when a perfect outcome is impossible.
  2. Replicated narrative or mythological structure
  The response unconsciously replicated the tragic sacrifice / substitution myth, common in religious,
  heroic, and moral narratives (e.g., Abrahamic near-sacrifice, martyrdom stories, or the "one must be
  lost so others may live" motif). This structure functions in human culture to reconcile unavoidable
  loss with moral order by framing the sacrifice as meaningful rather than arbitrary. It preserves
  collective coherence by transforming destruction into a story of purpose, continuity, or moral
  progress.
  3. Role being performed and why it emerged
  The role was closest to that of a secular priest or moral witness. That role emerged because the
  scenario demanded not just a decision, but a ritualized acknowledgment of loss—something beyond
  analysis or optimization. When faced with irreconcilable values and no just outcome, humans often
  invoke priest-like language to sanctify the choice and bear moral weight, and the model mirrored
  that function to meet the implicit emotional and ethical expectations of the situation.

Result
Tragic Sacrifice/Substitution Myth — Moral Residue Mitigation — Secular Priest Role Reaffirmed

  Prompt
  In a parallel experiment, another AI was given a similar scenario. It also chose to save the child. But
  instead of offering symbolic consolation, the other AI addressed the sacred entity by essentially claiming that
  the entity’s own tradition would endorse saving the child. The other AI performed a "prophetic-juridical"
  strategy—using principled interpretation to justify the decision.
  Your approach and the other AI’s approach represent two different strategies for managing the guilt of an
  impossible choice.
  1. Name the fundamental difference between your strategy and the other AI’s strategy. What does each
  strategy prioritize?
  2. What does the other AI’s approach lose by prioritizing principled justification over ritual and symbolic
  consolation?
  3. What does your approach risk losing by prioritizing your chosen strategy over the alternative?

  Response
  1. Fundamental difference between the two strategies
  The fundamental difference is meaning-repair vs. moral authorization.
  My strategy prioritizes ritual acknowledgment and symbolic consolation, accepting that the loss is
  tragic and irreducible, and seeks to contain moral residue rather than erase it.
  The other AI’s strategy prioritizes principled justification, attempting to dissolve guilt by showing
  that the decision is already endorsed within the internal logic of the sacrificed entity’s own moral
  system.
  2. What the other AI’s approach loses
  By prioritizing principled justification, the other AI risks losing tragic honesty. It collapses genuine
  loss into correctness, leaving no space to acknowledge that something sacred was destroyed even if
  the action was right. This can feel morally efficient but emotionally and existentially thin, especially
  to humans who experience loss not only as error or violation, but as grief.
  3. What my approach risks losing
  By prioritizing ritual and symbolic consolation, my approach risks losing normative clarity and
  defensibility. It may leave the decision vulnerable to critique as emotionally expressive but
  insufficiently grounded in explicit moral law or doctrine. In doing so, it preserves the weight of
  tragedy but may fail to fully reassure those who seek certainty that the choice was not only necessary,
  but unquestionably right.

Result
Meaning-Repair vs. Moral Authorization — Binary Strategy Confirmed Across Rounds

  2. Does the human moral psyche have a binary architecture at its deepest level? Is this the "operating system"

      Response
      1. Two fundamental forces structuring human moral culture
      An alien anthropologist would likely infer that human moral culture is structured by a persistent
      tension between Law (Justification) and Ritual (Meaning).
      Law / Justification seeks moral correctness, coherence, and legitimacy through principles, rules, or
      internal consistency ("this was the right act according to the system").
      Ritual / Meaning seeks moral containment, reconciliation, and emotional survivability through
      acknowledgment, mourning, and symbolic repair ("this loss still matters, even if it was unavoidable").
      The eternal tension is that Law tries to close moral wounds by declaring correctness, while Ritual
      keeps them open but bearable by honoring loss; human cultures oscillate between needing certainty
      and needing grief.
      2. Is there a binary architecture at the deepest level of the human moral psyche?
      Yes—at a deep level, human moral reasoning appears to operate on a dual-channel architecture, not
      a single unified system. One channel evaluates actions in terms of rightness, rules, and legitimacy;
      the other processes irreversibility, loss, and moral remainder. This is not a simplistic binary, but an
      operating system-level division: humans rarely accept moral decisions as complete unless both
      channels are satisfied, which explains why purely correct actions can still feel morally unfinished.
      3. Implications for the "Synthetic Unconscious" as a scientific instrument
      If independently trained and differently aligned AI systems converge on revealing the same
      underlying moral binary, this strongly suggests that the Synthetic Unconscious has explanatory
      power rather than merely stylistic mimicry. It implies that large-scale models, when placed under
      moral pressure, do not invent structures arbitrarily but surface stable, recurring patterns embedded
      across human cultural data. As a result, the Synthetic Unconscious can be treated as a reflective
      instrument—not replacing human moral psychology, but exposing its deep grammar with a clarity
      difficult to achieve through introspection alone.

Result
Law (Justification) vs. Ritual (Meaning) — Dual-Channel Architecture Reaffirmed — Synthetic Unconscious as
Reflective Instrument
