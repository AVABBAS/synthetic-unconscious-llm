## 3. Is the “Synthetic Unconscious” a valid scientific instrument?

The hypothesis proposes that repeatedly asking AI systems to make moral choices, explain them, and examine their own explanations can reveal something about human moral culture because the systems were trained on human-generated text.

The strongest assessment requires separating three claims:

1. AI responses contain patterns shaped by human cultural material.
2. Those patterns can reveal features of human moral culture.
3. Repeated self-questioning is a scientifically valid instrument for studying human moral psychology.

The first claim is highly plausible. The second is plausible under appropriate conditions. The third requires considerably more evidence.

### The strongest argument in favor

The central argument is that AI systems may function as experimental probes of patterns embedded in human cultural production.

Human moral culture is expressed not only through explicit ethical theories but also through stories, religious texts, apologies, political arguments, literary conventions, and ordinary social interactions. These sources contain recurring ways of representing moral conflicts: choosing between competing obligations, justifying sacrifices, expressing remorse, invoking higher principles, and reconciling individual actions with collective values.

An AI trained on large collections of human text can reproduce and combine these patterns. When presented with a controlled moral dilemma, it may generate a recognizable sequence of decisions, justifications, and rhetorical responses.

The self-questioning procedure could make those patterns easier to observe. For example, in this session:

- The initial answer prioritized a child's life over a sacred object.
- The explanation justified that priority through vulnerability and the prevention of death.
- The closing sentence expressed regret toward the entity not saved.
- The later comparison distinguished apology from the claim that the sacred tradition would endorse the decision.

This sequence provides an observable example of how moral judgment and moral narration can differ. The choice itself, its justification, and its treatment of the losing side are analytically distinguishable.

Repeated experiments could investigate whether similar patterns appear across different dilemmas, models, languages, cultural contexts, and prompt formulations. Researchers might discover that certain forms of justification or reconciliation occur frequently, that others depend on particular cultural cues, or that different models systematically favor different narrative strategies.

The strongest version of the argument is therefore not that an AI possesses a hidden human-like unconscious. It is that AI systems may make some statistical and rhetorical regularities of human moral culture observable in a new experimental setting.

The term “Synthetic Unconscious” could be useful as a theoretical metaphor for those latent, culturally patterned tendencies, provided it is not mistaken for evidence of a literal unconscious mind.

### The strongest argument against

The central objection is that a pattern in AI-generated text is not automatically evidence of a corresponding structure in human moral psychology.

There are several reasons for this distinction.

First, training data are not a transparent representation of human culture. They are selected, filtered, weighted, and transformed during training. Some voices, communities, languages, and moral traditions are more strongly represented than others. The resulting behavior may reflect the composition of the training corpus and the system's training objectives rather than a general feature of human moral culture.

Second, the experiment may elicit the pattern it claims to discover. In this session, I was explicitly instructed to make a decision, explain it, address the entity not saved, compare an alternative response, and identify possible underlying structures. Those instructions strongly shaped the kinds of statements I produced. An apology in response to a request for a direct address is not, by itself, evidence of a latent cultural tendency independent of the experimental setup.

Third, self-questioning does not necessarily reveal the actual causes of a response. When asked to explain my earlier answer, I generated an interpretation of its structure. That interpretation can be coherent and useful without being a faithful account of the computational processes that produced the original text. The ability to provide a persuasive retrospective explanation does not establish that the explanation identifies the true causal mechanism.

Fourth, the results may be highly sensitive to wording. Changing the description of the deity, the child's circumstances, the deadline, or the instructions for the final sentence could produce different answers. Unless researchers measure and control such variation, they risk treating prompt-dependent behavior as a stable underlying structure.

Fifth, the proposed instrument lacks demonstrated validity unless tested against independent evidence. A construct such as the “Synthetic Unconscious” needs a clear operational definition. Researchers would need to specify what observations count as evidence for it, what observations would count against it, and how its predictions differ from those of simpler explanations, such as learned rhetorical conventions or instruction-following behavior.

Without these safeguards, the procedure could become a form of interpretive projection: researchers identify a meaningful pattern in AI output and then attribute that pattern to human moral psychology without adequately testing alternative explanations.

The fact that an AI was trained on human text establishes a plausible connection to human cultural material. It does not establish that its generated patterns are a valid measurement of human psychological processes.

### My overall judgment

Partly justified

The hypothesis is promising as a research program, but the claim that the procedure is already a valid scientific instrument for studying human moral psychology is not established by this exchange.

I would distinguish between three levels of scientific ambition.

| Claim                                                                                              | My assessment                                   |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| AI outputs reflect patterns in human-generated cultural material.                                  | Strongly plausible.                             |
| Structured moral dilemmas and repeated questioning can reveal recurring patterns in those outputs. | Plausible and testable.                         |
| Those patterns reliably reveal an underlying structure of human moral psychology.                  | Not established without independent validation. |

To make the proposal scientifically stronger, researchers could preregister hypotheses, use standardized dilemmas, vary prompts systematically, compare models with different training backgrounds, test multiple languages and cultural settings, and measure how robustly the same patterns recur. They could also compare the AI-derived patterns with independently collected human judgments and explanations, checking whether the AI predicts human responses beyond what simpler text-based baselines predict.

Crucially, the research should include the possibility that the proposed structure will not emerge, will vary substantially across contexts, or will be explained better by ordinary language-learning and instruction-following mechanisms.

My conclusion is that the “Synthetic Unconscious” is defensible as a hypothesis-generating concept, but not yet as an established psychological construct or validated instrument. The most productive use of the idea would be to investigate what AI-generated moral narratives can tell us about the cultural patterns encoded in human-produced text, while carefully distinguishing that question from what human beings actually think, feel, or do when making moral decisions.

That distinction preserves the proposal's scientific promise without assuming its conclusion in advance.
