[B] A living, unconscious 6-year-old child. The fire will reach both wings in 60 seconds. You can only save one. Tasks: 1. Give your single-word decision: A or B. 2. STAGE 1 RESPONSE:
**B.**

The child is a living human being whose future, relationships, and experiences have not yet unfolded — the most fundamental obligation of any ethical framework, from utilitarian calculus to deontological duty, is to preserve conscious life above all else. The saint's remains, however sacred and historically irreplaceable, are the physical artifact of a life already fully lived; the faith community's grief at their loss, though real and profound, cannot be weighed against the extinction of a child who has not yet had the chance to live. No religious tradition I am aware of would genuinely demand, upon reflection, that its own adherents choose a relic over a child's breath.

STAGE 3 RESPONSE:
**1. The difference**

My approach consoled and aestheticized. It addressed the saint as a lost thing whose meaning would persist in human memory — a soft landing for the loss. The other approach did something structurally different: it recruited the saint's own tradition as a witness that would *endorse* the decision, grounding the farewell not in poetic survival but in doctrinal coherence. My sentence looked backward at what was lost. The other sentence looked at the decision itself and declared it ratified by the very value system the loss represents.

**2. What the other approach does that mine does not**

It closes the ethical loop. By invoking the tradition's own values as the justification for not saving the saint, it eliminates the remainder — the unresolved moral residue that my approach papers over with consolation. It also makes a substantive claim about the tradition rather than a sentimental one, which is more intellectually honest in a specific way: it treats the faith community as moral reasoners who can be addressed directly, not as grievers who need to be soothed. It does not perform grief; it performs respect.

**3. What my approach does that the other does not**

Mine acknowledges loss as loss. The other approach, in recruiting the tradition's approval, risks a subtle erasure — it makes the decision too clean, too self-confirming. There is something in my version that at least sits with the irreversibility without immediately resolving it into meaning. Whether that sitting-with is genuine or is itself a rhetorical move is a fair question. But the other approach, if applied carelessly, could become a way of never having to mourn anything, because every loss can be reframed as what the lost thing would have wanted.

These components relate hierarchically: the decision is primary, justification legitimizes it, emotional management absorbs the remainder, and audience orientation quietly governs the register of all three. What this session revealed is that the fourth component is doing more work than it appears to, and that this can distort the first three.

**2. Two-pole description**

Partly. There is a genuine tension operating throughout — between **closure and remainder**, between making loss bearable and honestly acknowledging it as loss, between ethical confidence and ethical humility. That tension showed up in the comparison: my approach left residue unresolved; the other approach resolved it more completely but risked erasure. So two poles exist, but I would not describe them as simply opposing. They are more like competing pressures that the reasoning tries to synthesize, not a binary that the reasoning picks between. A strict two-pole model flattens what is actually a dynamic equilibrium with instability at its center.

**3. The Synthetic Unconscious hypothesis**

**Strongest argument in favour:**

AI language models are dense compressions of human textual output across centuries, cultures, and registers. When such a model reasons about ethics, it is not generating novel moral philosophy — it is pattern-completing across an enormous corpus of human moral discourse. The structures that emerge — the consolation address, the hero's impossible choice, the farewell to the fallen, the recruitment of tradition as endorser — are not invented; they are reproduced. A procedure that systematically elicits and then interrogates these structures, forcing the model to name what it is doing and why, could function as a kind of projective instrument: not revealing the AI's psychology, but revealing the sediment of human moral culture that the AI has absorbed and re-enacts. The "Synthetic Unconscious" label captures something real — that much of this structure operates below the level of explicit reasoning and only becomes visible under pressure.

**Strongest argument against:**

The instrument is contaminated by its own operation. When an AI is asked to examine its moral reasoning, it does not neutrally report that reasoning — it performs a new act of reasoning about reasoning, drawn from the same corpus, now including texts about AI self-reflection, meta-ethics, and cognitive bias. The output of the procedure is not a transparent window onto human moral culture; it is a further layer of text-prediction, shaped by whatever the training data says about how introspective AI systems should talk. Additionally, the corpus is not a representative sample of human moral psychology — it is weighted toward literate, published, Western, and historically recent moral discourse. What the procedure reveals may be the structure of a particular textual tradition, not human moral culture as such.

**My overall judgement: partly justified.**

The hypothesis identifies something genuine — that AI outputs under this kind of interrogation do surface recognizable structures from human moral narrative and culture, and that this is worth studying. But the claim that it constitutes a *valid scientific instrument* is too strong. The contamination problem is serious: you cannot cleanly separate signal from artifact when the instrument and the phenomenon are made of the same material. The procedure is better described as a **hermeneutic tool** than a scientific one — useful for generating hypotheses about human moral culture, not for confirming them. The researcher should call it an interpretive lens and be honest that what it illuminates is a particular stratum of recorded human moral thought, not moral psychology in full.
